Atlas of European Collectives

Three commonwealths, thirteen facets

Abstract

This atlas identifies and empirically measures the attributes of a peoplehood, clusters them statistically, tests every result for robustness, and applies the method to European and European-derived populations. Fifty-four regions, from Iceland to Russia and Israel, with the settler societies and Ibero-America, are scored on thirteen facets, each resting on a published measurement or a documented classification.

Every facet is received at birth; they differ in what can happen afterwards. Two cannot change, even in principle: ancestry, measured as steppe, farmer and hunter-gatherer shares from ancient-DNA models, since gene editing can alter genes but not descent; and the ecoregion of one's birthplace, which no change of citizenship undoes and which passports and civil registers record for life. Eleven are inherited the same way but kept only by practice, so they can be taken up by people not born to them and lost by people who were: language, confession, script, legal family, family and kinship, learning, civic trust, individualism, architecture, foodways and the festive calendar.

For every pair of regions, each facet gives a distance from 0 to 1. Facets are scaled to equal spread, and each facet's weight is discounted by how far it repeats the others, so that correlated facets share one voice. The combined distances are clustered by average linkage, which reproduces them with a cophenetic correlation of 0.84, and the result is tested against other weights, other linkage rules, perturbed and shuffled data, and held-out facets.

Results. The clearest division separates the two Hebraic units, Israel and the Ashkenazi diaspora, from all others. The next divides the remainder into a Latin Roman West of 36 regions and a Byzantine East of 16. The same three groups emerge with both birthright facets given no weight. Built without any one facet and its close correlates, the groups still separate that facet's values for eleven of the thirteen facets, beyond what geographic distance explains; the exceptions are geographic descent and legal family. No shuffled dataset produces comparable structure (p < 0.005).

The cores are stable; the borders are not. Under 300 moderate re-weightings, each commonwealth overlaps its reference membership by 0.83 to 0.97 on average (Jaccard), but the exact three-way split recurs in only 90 draws. The regions that change sides are Croatia, Slovenia, Ibero-America, Romania, Poland and the Baltic states, where the church-related facets and the measured kinship, trust and individualism facets disagree. Clustered on the birthright facets alone, the regions sort into climate bands rather than into the three groups; foodways is the only transmitted facet that tracks ancestry.

        Why "commonwealth"

        After Dimitri Obolensky's The Byzantine Commonwealth (1971): peoples bound by a shared religious, legal and literary inheritance, not by a state or by blood. Under the Ottomans the Orthodox church went on administering the Balkans — Iorga's Byzance après Byzance — which is why Muslim Albania and Romance-speaking Romania sit in the Byzantine core.

        How the boundaries were drawn

        The atlas divides Europe and its offshoots into fifty-four units. Units follow cultural regions rather than modern states where the difference matters: Bohemia rather than Czechia; Bavaria, Swabia, the Rhineland and North Germany; the Mezzogiorno and Sardinia split from northern Italy; Galicia from the rest of Ukraine; Ulster from the rest of Ireland; Québec from English Canada. Boundaries are drawn from Natural Earth, a free public-domain map dataset. Country outlines use its medium-detail edition; where a unit is part of a country rather than a whole one, its provinces are taken from the high-detail edition and merged into a single shape — Bavaria from the German states, the Mezzogiorno from the southern Italian regions, Galicia from three Ukrainian oblasts. The United States, English Canada, Québec, Australia, New Zealand and Israel appear as insets. Two units, Ibero-America and the Ashkenazi diaspora, are not territorial and appear as swatches rather than map regions.

        How the sub-national regions were drawn, and why that matters. The splits inside Germany, Italy, the United Kingdom, Ukraine and Canada were drawn by hand before any clustering, following historic confessional, legal and linguistic boundaries that were already known to differ. That risks circularity: an atlas that claims to derive cultural regions should not assume them. Two consequences follow. First, the result was tested without them: leaving out each country's split regions, or all fourteen at once, leaves the remaining regions where they were, except that without Italy's three Spain and Portugal move (see Testing stability). These are also the regions that take their country's survey and food figures, so the same test covers that. Second, the regions themselves should be derived rather than drawn; that is the next revision.

        Facets

        Thirteen facets, in two kinds. Every one of them is received at birth: no one chooses their genes, their birthplace, their mother tongue or the church they are baptised into. The difference lies in what can happen afterwards. Birthright facets are facts of origin, settled once and beyond the reach of any later act: ancestry is fixed at conception, and a birthplace stays one's birthplace wherever one moves. Transmitted facets are inherited the same way but kept only by practice. An adult can learn another language, convert, live under another legal order or take up another household's customs, and a family can lose all of these within two or three generations. This is the test Heidegger sets out in Being and Time §74 — handed down rather than possessed, joined by taking up, losable — and it can be checked against the record: every transmitted facet has documented cases of adoption and of loss, and neither birthright facet has any. The clustering is run with the birthright facets in, and again with them at zero. Each facet rests on a published measurement or a documented classification, named in its category card.

        The two kinds make each other over time. Descent carries culture: the steppe migrations brought Indo-European speech, and the Ashkenazim kept Yiddish and halakha by marrying within the community. Culture shapes descent: dairying selected for adults who can digest milk, which is why the trait is commonest in north-western Europe, and marriage rules decided who had children with whom, so that in the Netherlands Catholics and Protestants remain genetically distinguishable. What the atlas separates is the scale, not the origin. Within one life ancestry is settled, and only the transmitted facets can still be taken up or refused; the switch-off test asks which kind draws the present lines, not which came first. The data show the cycle where it should appear: Foodways is the one transmitted facet that tracks ancestry.

        Birthright in law

        Birthright facets are settled by factors outside anyone's will. Most states confer citizenship on one of these grounds — by birthplace, as in the United States, or by parentage, as across much of Europe. Few extend it by descent from a people rather than from a citizen; Israel's Law of Return is the most prominent case.

        Measuring difference

        Each facet gives every pair of regions a distance from 0, identical, to 1, as different as possible. Shares are compared by how much of one would have to move to make the other, and where the categories form a family tree, by how far apart on it they sit: two Slavic languages differ less than a Slavic and a Germanic one. Ancestry uses log-ratios, the correct geometry for shares that sum to one hundred.

        Weighting and clustering

        Two steps put the facets on one footing. Each facet is divided by its own spread, so that a unit of difference means the same on every facet. Then each facet's voice is divided by one plus its summed positive correlation with the others: facets that repeat one another share a voice, and a facet that agrees with nothing keeps a full one. The voices come from the data, not from a hand-set weighting. The combined distances are clustered by average linkage, which keeps them best of the standard rules (cophenetic correlation 0.84, against 0.79 for complete and single linkage and 0.63 for Ward's). The tree is read from the top until France, Russia and Israel fall in different groups: the three anchors name the groups and set the depth, and never move a region.

        Formulas, procedure and the correlation matrix

        Every facet's distance runs from 0 to 1, but that does not make the facets equal. What a facet contributes to the clustering is its weight times how much its distances vary from pair to pair, and the facets vary very differently: Foodways gives a different distance for almost every pair but they bunch tightly, while Legal family takes a handful of values far apart. So, first, each facet is divided by its own spread, its standard deviation across the pairs where it exists, so that a unit of difference means the same on every facet.

        The second step is the voice each facet gets, and it is where an atlas can quietly decide its own result. Version 1.3 weighted each facet by how much it agreed with the others, which rewarded repetition. Version 1.4 gave every facet an equal voice, which still lets several facets that record one event outvote the rest. Since version 1.5 the atlas discounts repetition. Each facet's voice is divided by one plus its summed positive correlation with the others, so facets that repeat one another share a voice, and a facet that agrees with nothing keeps a full one. No facet is singled out by hand: the correlations, and therefore the voices, come from the data. Equal voices and the version 1.3 weights are kept as tests.

        The units are grouped by average-linkage hierarchical clustering: every unit starts alone, and the two closest groups merge again and again until one remains. Cutting that tree at different heights gives broader or finer groupings. No clustering rule is right in the abstract — every one returns groups, even from noise — so the choice is judged by two things: how faithfully the tree keeps the distances, and whether the groups survive tests they could fail. Average linkage keeps the distances best of the standard rules (a cophenetic correlation of 0.84, against 0.79 for complete and single linkage and 0.63 for Ward's method); it does not assume the distances are Euclidean, as Ward's method does; and it neither chains through outliers, as single linkage does, nor is ruled by them, as complete linkage is.         How the facets corroborate one another

        Agreement between two facets counts as corroboration only if they arrived by separate routes; if one carried the other, it is one fact recorded twice. Each of the seventy-eight pairs is classed from the sources as dependence, hypothesis, common cause, coincidence or independent. A facet is flagged when it agrees with another at r ≥ 0.5, or when more than half of it can be predicted from the rest. A flag prompts a decision (merge, split, or keep with a reason), not a verdict. The open flags are Confession with Script, Art and the calendar, which follow the churches, and Family & kinship with Confession, which is the Goody–Henrich hypothesis itself and so is kept apart to be tested.

        All seventy-eight pairs, with Mantel tests

        Two facets that draw the same line can mean two different things. If they arrived by separate routes — a legal system and a building style, each carried by different people for different reasons — their agreement is corroboration, and it should count. If one carried the other, or both were carried by one institution, their agreement is the same fact recorded twice, and it should not. Statistics can measure the agreement; only history can say which kind it is. So every pair of the thirteen facets — seventy-eight pairs — is listed below with its correlation r, a Mantel permutation test of that correlation, and the link between the two as the sources describe it: dependence, where one is carried by the other or both by one institution; hypothesis, a causal link the literature proposes and this atlas can test but not prove; common cause, where both follow a third thing, usually geography or wealth; coincidence, agreement with no known mechanism; and independent.

        The Mantel p-value is the share of 999 random relabellings of the regions that produce agreement at least as strong as the real one. It is computed after the page loads, so the column fills in a moment after the rest.

        What the results show

        Chance and method. No shuffle comes near the real structure. Average, single and Ward's linkage draw the same West–East line, Ward's moving only Ibero-America; complete linkage, which fits the distances worst but one, also moves Israel and the Ashkenazim.

        Prediction. The groups predict eleven of the thirteen facets held out of them, net of nearness. The two that fail are Geographic descent, a climate map, and Legal family, which crosses every other line.

        Re-weighting. Across 300 moderate draws each commonwealth keeps most of its members in nearly every draw, but the exact three-way split reproduces in fewer than a third; the regions that move are Croatia, Slovenia, Ibero-America, Romania, the Baltic states and Poland.

        Leaving facets out. Without the calendar the West–East line falls apart. Without Confession or Script alone it holds, because each carries most of the other. Without Legal family, Family & kinship, Learning, Individualism or Foodways, Croatia and Slovenia return to the West; without Art, Ibero-America does.

        Methodological risks

        1. Two facets are surveys of the present. Trust and individualism rise with wealth and the rule of law; without them Croatia, Slovenia, Romania and Ibero-America move West.
        2. Coverage is uneven. The Ethnographic Atlas codes 21 regions and Hofstede misses 8 countries; gaps are left out, not guessed.
        3. National figures stand in for regions, which adds agreement among facets coded by country.
        4. The West–East line is the churches' line. The other facets alone split north from south.
        5. Units are counted, not people. Germany is four units; the Hebraic group is only two.
        All eleven risks, in full
        1. Two of the new facets are surveys of the present. Trust and individualism are measured by asking people questions now, and both rise with income, education and the rule of law, which the atlas does not measure. Part of what they record is the Iron Curtain and a century of economic history, not a heritage. They are kept because individualism is the trait most often claimed for the West and because the test without them is on the page: without the two surveys, Croatia, Slovenia, Romania and Ibero-America move to the Latin Roman West and the Baltic states to the Byzantine East.
        2. Measured, but unevenly. The Ethnographic Atlas codes only twenty-one of the regions, through one society each, and its European entries are coarse: the Russians are coded as of 1955, after collectivisation. Hofstede has no score for eight countries. The books series covers twelve. Each missing figure is left out rather than guessed, so the family facet for Slovakia or Bosnia is the Hajnal line alone, and the learning facet for Slovenia is one founding date.
        3. National figures for regions. Survey, food, schooling and cousin-marriage figures are national, so Germany's four regions, Italy's three, Britain's three and Canada's two share them, which adds agreement with any other facet also coded by country. Bavaria and the Rhineland have their own religious shares and holidays; they do not have their own trust or individualism.
        4. The line is the churches' line. Clustered only on the facets that do not follow the churches — ancestry, landscape, language, law, learning, trust, individualism and food — the regions split north from south instead, and the transmitted ones among them draw something nearer the Hajnal line. The West–East division is carried by Confession, Script, Art, the calendar and, in part, Family & kinship. The prediction test shows that the groups those facets help draw also sort the facets they were not built from; it does not show that the other facets would draw the same line on their own. They would not.
        5. The discount trades one risk for another. Discounting repetition stops one event from being counted several times, but it hands the largest voices to the facets that agree with little — Legal family, Geographic descent and Learning — and a facet that agrees with little can be signal or noise.
        6. The coding that remains is judgement. Art and Geographic descent are still categories assigned from sources, the Hajnal line is a line drawn on a map, and each choice of society to stand for a region in the Ethnographic Atlas is a call. No statistic can test those calls, and the seam regions are exactly where a different call would move a region.
        7. Unequal, overlapping units. The clustering counts units, not people. Germany is four units, the settler societies are five near-copies of England and France, and the Hebraic Jews are only two units, which on the headline voices are further from each other than many pairs in different commonwealths. A group of two is a pair of distinctive outliers, not a demonstrated civilisation-scale bloc.
        8. The finer structure is soft. The sub-groups are the best-scoring cut below the commonwealths, and their stability scores, shown in the cards in Plate IV, range from firm to dissolving. Treat them as readings.
        9. Rough ancestry inputs. The ancestry shares are rounded from three-way models, and many sub-national regions inherit their country's figures. A systematic bias — a reference panel that misreads the whole Balkans, say — would not show up as noise.
        10. Pairs are not independent. The 1,431 pairs are built from 54 units, so an ordinary significance test of a correlation between facets would overstate it. The Mantel permutation test in the pair table is the correct one: it relabels whole regions, not pairs.
        11. The frame. Which regions are in the atlas decides what counts as close. With Anatolia, the Caucasus or the Maghreb added, the Eastern and Hebraic groups might absorb or lose members, and every measured facet would show Europe's distance from its neighbours, which the atlas cannot yet see. The commonwealths are a finding about these fifty-four units.

        Biological

        Ancient ancestral make-up: the blood half of blood and soil

        The Biological facet is measured by one thing, ancient ancestry, taken from the published ancient-DNA models that decompose every European population into three founding migrations.

        The three founding populations

        Almost everyone in Europe descends from the same three populations, in different proportions. Modern humans first reached Europe about 45,000 years ago, but those earliest groups left little or no ancestry in anyone alive today. Western hunter-gatherers, the first of the three, descend from populations that spread across the continent after the last Ice Age, from about 14,000 years ago. Anatolian farmers arrived out of what is now Turkey from about 7000 BCE with agriculture, pottery and permanent settlement, and largely absorbed them. Yamnaya steppe herders arrived from the Pontic-Caspian grasslands from about 3000 BCE with Indo-European speech, horses and wheeled carts, in a movement so male-biased that in Britain the Beaker horizon replaced roughly ninety per cent of the gene pool within a few centuries.

        The mixture was essentially settled by 2000 BCE. Everything the transmitted facets describe is Iron Age at the earliest and mostly medieval, which puts this facet three millennia too early to be causing any of it.

        Dialectics

        Punjab. Steppe ancestry is not a European property. Narasimhan and colleagues traced the same Bronze-Age steppe component into South Asia, where it is substantial in Indo-Aryan-speaking populations. A Norwegian and a Punjabi share an ancestral source that a Sardinian largely lacks. If this facet defined a people, that people would run from Bergen to Bengal.
        Share is not contribution. A population that moves into occupied land and interbreeds even slightly at its leading edge absorbs local genes as it advances, until it is mostly local itself. Reich (2026) argues that this is how the Neanderthals formed. An early modern human population spread into Europe about 300,000 years ago and ended only 3 to 7 per cent modern by ancestry. Yet it kept its mitochondrial and Y-chromosome lineages whole, and it may have carried Levallois tools, controlled fire, ochre and hafting across the continent. Bronze Age Iberia shows the same signature on a smaller scale: steppe-related ancestry reached about 40 per cent of the genome, while the Y chromosomes were replaced almost entirely. This facet measures share. It cannot say whose ways were handed on, and that is the question every other facet asks.

        Heidegger's test

        Fails. Geschick requires a heritage that can be taken up as a task and lost by not taking it up. Nobody can take up an ancestry, and nobody can lose one: it is settled at conception and carried whether or not anyone attends to it. Descent is a Kategorie applicable to things present-at-hand, and Dasein is not analysed through those.

        Why it is included

        Descent is the oldest answer to the question this atlas asks, and the one most often used to define a European peoplehood, so it has to be measured rather than assumed. Ancient-DNA studies now give that measurement in a form no earlier generation had: three founding populations, in proportions that can be compared region by region. The facet is in the atlas so that the claim that descent organises peoplehood can be tested on its best evidence.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Ancestry shares are compositional: they must sum to one hundred, so a rise in one component forces a fall elsewhere, and ordinary distances between percentages are distorted, worst near zero. The standard treatment, due to John Aitchison, is to take the centred log-ratio of the three shares and measure distance in that space; that is the Biological distance used in the clustering. For the maps, each region is placed on the main axis of variation in those log-ratios — in practice a gradient from farmer-rich to steppe-rich — and coloured along it, dark for farmer-rich, pale blue for steppe-rich.

        An earlier version also measured phenotype, eye colour and stature, as a second sub-division. It was dropped because its figures were the softest in the atlas, and because stature measures nutrition as much as descent: Dutch men gained twenty centimetres in five generations on an unchanged gene pool.

        Further cases

        Ötzi and the Sardinians. The Iceman, who died in the Alps around 3300 BCE, clusters genetically with modern Sardinians. Sardinia is the closest living population to the Neolithic farmers, which makes it the most genetically continuous place in Europe — and the least steppe. Whatever "deep European" means, it points at Cagliari, not Uppsala.

        Geographic descent

        Blood and soil, the soil half: the living landscape a people is born into

        The frame for this facet is Alfred W. Crosby's Ecological Imperialism: The Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900 (1986). Crosby asked why Europeans became the majority population in North America, the Southern Cone, Australia and New Zealand, but nowhere in tropical Africa or Asia. His answer was ecological, not cultural: Europeans succeeded where the land already resembled home — temperate forest and grassland in the same latitudes — and they did not arrive alone. They brought a "portmanteau biota": wheat and barley, cattle, sheep, pigs and horses, the weeds that followed the plough, and the Old World diseases that emptied the land ahead of them. Where that whole package could take root, it remade the landscape into what Crosby called Neo-Europes; where it could not, Europeans ruled but never became the people. The biome decided where Europe could be reproduced.

        Each region is coded by the biome in which most of its people are born, not the biome that covers most of its area. Russia is mostly taiga by area, but most Russians are born in the mixed forests between Moscow and the Volga; Israel is mostly Negev desert, but most Israelis are born on the Mediterranean coastal plain. Every biome present in a region, taken from the ecoregion table, is listed in its region card. In the distance measure the biome carries three quarters of the weight — with related biomes, such as the two temperate forests, taiga and tundra, or Mediterranean scrub and desert, counted as half as far apart — and the realm carries the remaining quarter.

        Crosby's own evidence also marks the limit of his thesis for this atlas. The biome explains where settlers could thrive; it does not explain what they carried. The Neo-Europes chose their biome by moving into it, and what crossed the ocean with the wheat and the cattle was the transmitted part — the language, the law, the church. The clustering bears that out: geographic descent agrees with biology, weakly with what a region eats, and with almost nothing else transmitted.

        Dialectics

        One biome, three commonwealths. Rome, Constantinople and Jerusalem all stand in the Mediterranean forest, woodland and scrub biome. So do Lisbon, Athens, Tirana and Cagliari. Weight the two birthright facets alone and the clustering puts Portugal, Spain, the Mezzogiorno, Sardinia, Montenegro, Albania, Greece and Israel into a single group: Latin, Byzantine and Hebraic together. All three commonwealths were born in the same biome, and nothing that divides them grew out of it.

        Heidegger's test

        Fails, though at first it seems to pass. Anyone can move into a biome, own land in it and leave it, but each time the subject is the person, not a heritage. Land passes between generations as property, not as a possibility to be repeated. Moving into a landscape is residence, not appropriation: what a newcomer actually takes up is the language, law and faith already there. And a biome can be lost to the plough or to warming without anyone failing at anything. Nothing is handed down, taken up or lost as a heritage, which is where Heidegger's own later talk of rootedness strays from his criterion.

        Why it is included

        Soil is the other half of blood and soil, and the strongest version of the claim — Crosby's, that Europe could be reproduced only where the land resembled it — is an empirical one. Coding the biome where a people is born, from an independent scientific classification, lets the atlas ask whether the landscape organises anything a people hands down.

        About the classification

        That is the strongest case anyone has made for soil as a condition of peoplehood, and the facet is built to test it. It asks where a people is born, measured not by borders but by the land itself, using the biogeographical system Miklos Udvardy drew up for UNESCO in 1975, as extended by the World Wide Fund for Nature. The Earth is divided into eight realms — the Palearctic, Nearctic, Neotropical, Australasian and others, each with its own lineage of plants and animals — and fourteen biomes, the kinds of living landscape a climate produces: temperate broadleaf forest, boreal taiga, Mediterranean scrub, steppe, desert. Within them sit some eight hundred ecoregions, from the Pannonian mixed forests to the Pontic steppe. On this facet the Anglosphere shares Europe's biome but not its realm: Crosby's Neo-Europes, visible on the map as the same forest on another continent.

        How the coding was done. Biome lists come from the Udvardy–WWF ecoregion table, at country level for whole-country units and hand-assigned from the parent country's ecoregions for sub-national ones such as Bavaria, the Mezzogiorno and Galicia; outlying islands off the map, such as the Azores, the Canaries and Hawaii, are excluded. The birthplace biome is a judgement from where each region's cities and population lie. Ibero-America is the union of Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Peru, Colombia and Chile. The Ashkenazim, having no territory, are coded by the Pale of Settlement where most of them were born until the twentieth century.

        Further cases

        Montesquieu, 1748. The Spirit of the Laws, Books XIV to XVIII, derived character and law from climate and soil: cold made northern peoples vigorous and free, heat made southern ones indolent and despotic. The atlas tests the proposition directly. Geographic descent agrees with biology — a correlation of 0.33 — and weakly with foodways, because climate decides what grows; with script, family, learning and legal family it agrees hardly at all, at about 0.05 or less, and with confession at 0.10. Climate does not write laws.
        Blut und Boden. The slogan paired blood with soil because both are birthrights, fixed before anyone can choose. Heidegger himself lamented the loss of Bodenständigkeit, rootedness in native soil, in his 1955 memorial address at Meßkirch. The atlas measures blood and soil separately and together, and finds that each agrees mostly with the other and neither organises anything a people hands down, except, loosely, what it eats.

        Heidegger's test, in full

        At first glance the biome seems to pass all three of Heidegger's tests: anyone can move to the landscape they prefer, anyone can leave it, and land can be handed down. It fails every one of them, and for the same reason each time: what moves, owns and leaves is the person, not a heritage.

        Transmitted, not possessed — fails. Land is the paradigm of what is possessed. It is held as private property under title, or as public land held by a state, a municipality or a crown, and when it passes between generations it passes as an estate: a thing, by inheritance law. The heritage of §74 is a store of possibilities to be repeated, not goods to be owned. What is genuinely handed down about land is the law of owning it — Roman dominium, title and conveyance, the public trust, the municipality as a legal person — and that belongs to Legal family, not to the soil.

        Membership by taking it up — fails. Anyone can move to a biome, and whole peoples have: that is how the Neo-Europes were settled and how Florida and Arizona filled. But moving into a landscape is residence, not appropriation. The biome asks nothing of the newcomer and offers no possibility to repeat; a Finn who retires to Mediterranean scrub is not thereby Mediterranean. What a migrant actually takes up on arrival is the language, law and faith of the people already there — the transmitted facets.

        Losable — fails, in the wrong sense. You can leave a biome, but then it is you who have gone, not a heritage that has lapsed; the biome carries on without you. And a biome can be lost without anyone leaving or failing at anything: the Pontic steppe was ploughed into cropland, Mediterranean forest degraded into scrub, and warming is moving the treeline and the edge of the steppe northward. Heidegger's losability is loss through failing to take a heritage up, a lapse into the anonymous "they". Loss that merely happens to people is not that.

        So the biome imitates each criterion by changing its subject: a person can enter, own and leave a biome, but nothing is handed down, taken up or lost as a heritage. It stays present-at-hand. §74's Geschick has no place for soil, which is exactly where Heidegger's own later talk of rootedness strays from his criterion.

        Language

        Fichte's criterion, and what happens when you run it

        Fichte delivered the Addresses to the German Nation in Berlin in the winter of 1807 and 1808, with French troops occupying the city. The argument is almost always mis-remembered as a blood argument. It is a language argument, and he is explicit about it.

        The claim: a people that has spoken its own tongue continuously since its origin possesses an Ursprache. Every abstract term in it still traces back through living metaphor to sensuous experience all its speakers have had. A people that adopted a foreign tongue instead — his case is the Romance nations taking Latin — received its abstract vocabulary pre-formed and severed at the root. Therefore the Germans are the Urvolk, and only a people with a living root can be genuinely philosophical and therefore genuinely free.

        The tell is his own membership rule. In the Seventh Address the criterion stops being linguistic at the decisive moment: whoever believes in spirituality and its freedom, and wills its endless progress, is one of this people, wherever born and whatever tongue they speak; whoever believes in stagnation and mechanism is foreign, even if born in Germany. That is a metaphysical test, open to anyone, far closer to Heidegger's §74 than to anything in Gobineau.

        The map colours each region by the branch of its majority language, which is why Ireland, Ulster and Scotland appear as West Germanic and Belgium, Luxembourg and Switzerland as multilingual. The distance uses every first language a region speaks, by its share.

        Dialectics

        Yiddish. A West Germanic language, structurally closer to German than English is, written in Hebrew characters. On Fichte's own criterion the Ashkenazim are inside the German language community. The sharpest demonstration available that a linguistic criterion cannot be made to draw an ethnic boundary, and it comes from inside the framework.

        Why it is included

        Language is the criterion Fichte made central and the one most nationalisms since have used. It is transmitted, taught and losable, and it divides populations that every other facet unites, so it is the clearest test of whether a single cultural marker can define a people.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Every first language a region speaks, by its share of the population, from the latest census or survey: the CIA World Factbook's country figures, Statistics Canada's 2021 census for Québec and the rest of Canada, and Israel's 2011 social survey, in which 49 per cent of adults gave Hebrew as their mother tongue, 18 Arabic and 15 Russian. Where no shares are published, the official majority language counts as the whole. Languages outside these families — Basque, Romani, the Caucasian languages, recent immigrant languages — are left out and the rest re-summed. Two regions are then compared on the family tree: as far apart as the deepest split between their speakers, so that Belgium, three-fifths West Germanic and two-fifths Romance, sits between the Netherlands and France rather than apart from both, and Latvia, a third East Slavic, sits partway toward Russia.

        Further cases

        English. Germanic in its grammar, function words and core vocabulary; roughly half Romance in its lexicon after Norman French and centuries of Latin borrowing. On Fichte's binary it is neither pure case, and it is the language of the whole Anglosphere inset.
        Hebrew. Nobody's mother tongue for roughly seventeen centuries, then revived as a spoken vernacular within two generations. If unbroken continuity of root is the test, Hebrew should be the deadest language on earth. It is instead the clearest case anywhere of a language taken up as a task and made living again by decision — Heidegger's criterion rather than Fichte's, and it beats Fichte's on his own ground. It is on the map now: Israel is the only Semitic cell in the atlas.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes, and Fichte very nearly reaches the criterion himself. A language is handed down, taken up by each generation, and genuinely losable — Ireland lost most of a vernacular within three generations and has spent a century deciding whether to take it back up, which is Wiederholung in the strict sense. Where Fichte breaks from §74 is in claiming one particular inheritance is metaphysically privileged. Heidegger's apparatus has no resources for that claim.

        Confession

        The quarrels that split the inheritance

        Confession divides Europe twice, and the two divisions are not the same kind of thing. The 1054 split turned on papal primacy and the Filioque — whether the Creed should say the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. That line maps almost exactly onto the alphabet boundary and onto the old Latin Roman coding, because all three are the same event seen from different angles.

        The Reformation is a different order of quarrel: scripture alone against scripture plus tradition, faith alone against faith formed by charity, two sacraments against seven. Arguments about how grace reaches a person, conducted in Latin by men trained in the same universities. Protestant polities kept canon-law marriage structure, corporate personality, the chartered university and the binding promise. The Greek Catholic value exists because of the Union of Brest in 1596: Byzantine rite, Latin jurisdiction. And the southeast corner of the map carries Islam — Albania's plurality and Bosnia's Bosniaks, the legacy of four centuries of Ottoman rule.

        Dialectics

        Bavaria and Saxony. Two populations genetics can barely tell apart, born in the same forests, under the same jurisdiction, legal family and alphabet, speaking dialects of the same language. What divides them arrived in 1517, is entirely a matter of doctrine, and has structured five centuries of politics, marriage and war. Its genetic trace, where one can be found at all, is faint.

        Why it is included

        Religion is the most widely used marker of collective identity in European history, and the branch of faith — not merely the religion — is what divided Europe in 1054 and again in 1517. It records which church converted a people; the alphabet that came with it is the next facet.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Each region holds shares of the population that belongs to a religion: Latin Catholic, Greek Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, other Christian, Muslim and Jewish. The national figures are the Pew Research Center's estimates for 2010; Germany's four regions are built from the Politbarometer's state figures for 2016, weighted by state population; Scotland, Northern Ireland and Québec take their censuses; Galicia takes the 2015 survey of its three oblasts, and the rest of Ukraine the national figure without them. People with no religion are left out, because the facet records which tradition a population belongs to, not how observant it is: most Czechs have no religion, and nine in ten of those who do are Catholic. The shares sit on a tree — Christianity, Islam and Judaism at the top, Western and Eastern Christianity below, Catholic and Protestant below that — so that Estonia, half Lutheran and nearly half Orthodox, sits between Finland and Russia, and Bosnia, 46 per cent Muslim, 39 Orthodox and 15 Catholic, sits between all three.

        Further cases

        Christ, across three traditions. For Christianity, Jesus is God incarnate, the second person of the Trinity, crucified and risen. For Islam, 'Isa is a prophet and the Messiah, born of a virgin, honoured — but not divine, and not crucified, since the Qur'an says it was made to appear so; and to say God has a son is shirk, the gravest sin. For Judaism, he is not the messiah, because the messianic age plainly did not arrive, and a triune God cannot be reconciled with the Shema. Three traditions sharing Abraham, prophets, strict monotheism and most of a moral vocabulary, dividing absolutely on one person.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes. Confession is handed down through named institutions, taken up or not by each person and each generation, and losable in the most direct way any facet here is losable. Confession also shows the vacancy in §74 as clearly as anything could: the criterion cannot tell a Bavarian which of the two inheritances available in 1517 was properly his.

        Script

        The letters a people reads and writes, and the church that brought them

        Where the alphabets came from

        In early medieval Europe almost everyone who could write was a cleric, and a people's first alphabet was the one its missionaries brought for scripture and liturgy. In 863 the Byzantine brothers Cyril and Methodius were sent to Moravia to preach to the Slavs in their own tongue, and built an alphabet for it — Glagolitic, from which their disciples in Bulgaria derived Cyrillic. The Latin church, meanwhile, carried the Latin alphabet with its liturgy. Where Rome won the conversion, Slavs write in Latin letters: Poles, Czechs, Slovaks, Slovenes, Croats. Where Constantinople won, they write in Cyrillic: Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Bulgarians, Macedonians, Serbs. Moravia itself is the natural experiment: the same Slavs began with a Slavonic alphabet, the Frankish Latin clergy then won their church, and Czech has been written in Latin letters ever since.

        The rule holds even across languages. Muslim Moriscos in Spain wrote Spanish in Arabic letters; the Karamanlides, Orthodox Christians who spoke Turkish, wrote Turkish in Greek letters; Jewish communities wrote German and Spanish in Hebrew script, as Yiddish and Ladino. And it follows the rite more precisely than the bishop: Galicia's Greek Catholics joined Rome and kept the Byzantine liturgy, and with it Cyrillic.

        The exceptions are state projects, not drift. Romania, Orthodox, moved from Cyrillic to Latin letters in the 1860s to proclaim a Latin identity; Atatürk moved Turkish to Latin script in 1928; the Soviet state imposed Cyrillic on Moldova, and Moldova returned to Latin letters in 1989. So the alphabet is largely a mark of confession, and version 1.5 folded the two into one facet. Since then every facet's voice has been discounted for what it repeats, so the two share a voice without being merged, and kept apart, the places where they part company — Romania and Moldova, Orthodox in Latin letters; Serbia, Bosnia and Montenegro, in both alphabets; Galicia, Greek Catholic in Cyrillic — are visible on a map of their own.

        Dialectics

        One language, two alphabets. Vuk Karadžić's reformed Cyrillic and Ljudevit Gaj's Latin alphabet were built in the first half of the nineteenth century to correspond letter for letter, and the Vienna Literary Agreement of 1850 set a common standard. The same sentence transliterates between them without loss. Croats chose one alphabet and Serbs the other along the confessional line; Serbia, Bosnia and Montenegro now use both. Here script separates what language cannot.

        Why it is included

        The alphabet is the most visible boundary in Europe — a traveller meets it on the first street sign — and it records which church converted a people and what it has read and written since. It stands beside Confession rather than inside it because the two part wherever a state intervened, and the voices already discount what they share.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Each region is coded by the alphabet in everyday public use — on street signs, in newspapers, in school. Where two are in everyday use, as in Serbia, Bosnia and Montenegro, each counts half. Alphabets are compared on their family tree, by the same rule as languages and confessions: every alphabet in the atlas descends from the Phoenician, Latin, Cyrillic and Greek through the Greek alphabet and Hebrew through Aramaic, so two regions in different alphabets of the Greek branch are half as far apart as either is from Hebrew.

        Further cases

        Fraktur, 1941. German was printed in blackletter for four centuries, and the Nazi state promoted it as the authentic German script. In January 1941 a circular from Martin Bormann abolished it, calling it "Schwabacher Jew-letters" — a genealogy invented on the spot to justify switching to roman type, which was more legible in occupied territory. The regime most committed to the Volk's own forms abolished the Volk's own script by memo, on a fabricated pedigree.
        Fichte's argument, applied to script.

        Put "archive" in place of "vocabulary" and Fichte's case transfers: a people that keeps its alphabet can read its own past, and one that switches is cut off from its written memory within a generation. Turks born a few years after the 1928 switch could not read their grandparents' documents. An alphabet is adopted or refused whole, at a datable moment, and it is defended at cost: the Lithuanian book smugglers carried Latin-letter books across the border for forty years rather than accept Cyrillic. But by Fichte's own continuity test every European script fails — Rome took its letters from the Greeks through the Etruscans, and Cyrillic was built for the Slavs by Greek missionaries — and an alphabet switched by decree cannot be constitutive, or Moldovans would have become a different people twice in fifty years.

        Script is the sign of an inheritance more than its source, which is why its voice is shared with Confession's rather than counted in full.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes. An alphabet is handed down through schools and presses, taken up by every child who learns to read, and losable by decree in a single year, as Romania, Moldova and Turkey each found.

        Legal family

        Where law comes from, and who is allowed to make it

        The old Latin Roman facet asked which legal persons and obligations exist at all; this one asks how law gets made, and since version 1.6 it also carries what that facet meant by Roman-law contract, because the civil law is the Roman law's direct descendant. In the civil law tradition the code is primary, judges apply rather than make, and earlier decisions do not bind. In the common law tradition decisions are the law: precedent binds, judges make law incrementally, and no code purports to be complete.

        From around 1500 the continent underwent the reception of Roman law. It largely stopped at the Channel, because the English royal courts had already spent three centuries building a working system out of writs and juries. Common law is a sibling inside the inheritance, not an exception to it. Mixed systems sit where the two met: Scotland, which kept a Roman-law foundation inside the United Kingdom, and Québec, which runs a French-derived Civil Code for private law and English common law for public law. The Nordic family is codified but grown from customary law rather than received Roman law, and halakha appears because the Ashkenazi row needs it: jurist-made law, with real courts, and no state behind any of it.

        Dialectics

        The PRC civil code, 2020. A modern, systematic civil code drawn from German and Soviet models, adopted deliberately and competently — without judicial independence, since the courts remain answerable to the Party. The cleanest demonstration that legal family and jurisdictional independence are separate variables.

        Why it is included

        How law is made — by code or by precedent — is a separate question from which legal persons exist, and it divides regions that the churches unite: England from France, Québec from the rest of Canada. It is the atlas's test of whether two branches of one inheritance behave as one facet or two.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Each region is placed in its legal family after the University of Ottawa's JuriGlobe classification: civil law, common law, mixed, or, for the Ashkenazim, halakha, with the Nordic countries as a branch of civil law after Zweigert and Kötz. Each region's card on Plate I names the code in force and the model it was drafted on, as evidence. They show what the old Latin Roman facet missed: Roman law is not a Latin marker. Byzantium kept it in better condition than the West; Greece applied Harmenopoulos's Byzantine Hexabiblos as its civil law until 1946; and Russia's Civil Code of 1994 is a pandectist code drafted on German and Dutch models. What was Latin was the second jurisdiction and the university that taught the law, which the atlas now measures elsewhere.

        Further cases

        Islamic fiqh. Structurally closer to common law than most Europeans expect: jurist-made rather than sovereign-made, developed by scholars with real doctrinal autonomy from the ruler, through argument over cases. What it lacks is binding precedent, an appellate hierarchy, and a corporate body issuing judgements.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes. A legal tradition is transmitted through named institutions, taken up by each generation of practitioners as a task, and genuinely losable — a jurisdiction that stops honouring precedent has left the common law however its statutes read.

        Family and kinship

        Whom you may marry, where you live afterwards, and whether your cousins are your clan

        The oldest argument about what made Europe different is about the family. Jack Goody argued in 1983 that the Western Church pressed rules on marriage that no other civilisation had: no marriage between cousins, at its height out to sixth cousins; no marriage to a dead husband's brother; no adoption of heirs; no second wife; and, from the twelfth century, marriage by the free consent of the two people, whatever their families wanted. The effect was to break up the extended kin groups that held land and loyalty. John Hajnal found its demographic trace in 1965: west of a line from Saint Petersburg to Trieste, Europeans married late, many never married at all, and couples set up their own households; east of it, as in most of the world, marriage was early, near-universal and into the husband's family. In 2019 Jonathan Schulz, Duman Bahrami-Rad, Jonathan Beauchamp and Joseph Henrich measured kinship intensity across the world's societies and linked it, country by country, to centuries of exposure to the Western Church and to individualism, trust and conformity today.

        Dialectics

        The Hajnal line and the church line. In the north they coincide: Lutheran Estonia and Latvia lie west of the line, Orthodox Russia east. In the Danube basin they part. Catholic Hungary, Slovakia and Croatia lie east of it, and the joint household of brothers and their families — the zadruga — was one institution on both sides of the Catholic–Orthodox border, in Croatia's Military Frontier as in Serbia, until Croatia's laws of 1874 and 1889 opened it to division. The Church's marriage rules applied in Zagreb as in Vienna; the household form did not follow them.

        Why it is included

        Canon-law marriage was the first of the four institutions of the old Latin Roman facet, and the one with the strongest modern evidence behind it. It is a facet of its own so that the claim can be tested rather than assumed: if the Church's marriage rules made the West, the family facet should draw the church line. It draws most of it, and leaves it in the Danube basin.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Three indicators, each where it exists, each put on a 0–1 scale by its range:

        • Kinship intensity. For the twenty-one regions the Ethnographic Atlas covers, the society that stands for the region is scored on the five codes Schulz and colleagues use — cousin marriage preferred, polygyny, extended households, lineages, and residence with the husband's kin — and the five are averaged. The Atlas's European entries are coarse, and the choice of society is a call: the Walloons stand for Belgium, the Neapolitans for the Mezzogiorno, the Ukrainians for both Ukraine and Galicia, the Gheg for Albania. The Icelanders, coded as of the year 1100, and the French Basques are left out as unrepresentative.
        • The Hajnal line. 0 west of the line, 1 east of it, ½ where the region's population lies astride it, as Poland's, Lithuania's and Slovenia's does. Outside Europe it is not defined.
        • Cousin marriage. The share of marriages between second cousins or closer, from Alan Bittles's global compilation. Every European country in it lies between 0.1 and 2 per cent; Israel's 20 per cent averages communities that marry very differently, for cousin marriage has long been common among its Arab citizens and in some Jewish communities from the Middle East.

        The distance is the mean difference over the indicators two regions share, so Slovakia and Bosnia, which have only the Hajnal line, are compared on that alone, and New Zealand and the Ashkenazim, which have none, are left out of this facet.

The marriage pattern that defines the West was not inherited from any ancestor; it was imposed by the Church, resisted for centuries, and finally taken up.

        Learning

        The chartered university, and what came after it: books and schools

        The chartered university — a self-governing corporation of masters and students with a licence to teach anywhere — is a Latin invention: Bologna from 1088, Paris about 1150, Oxford by 1167, then Salamanca, Prague, Kraków and Vienna before 1400. The Byzantine world had higher schools, some older than Bologna, but not the self-governing corporation, and Russia's first university dates from the eighteenth century. The facet asks how long the institution has stood in each region, and what followed it.

        Dialectics

        The Protestant north. Norway had no university until 1811, yet by 1910 its adults had more schooling than any other European country's, and in the eighteenth century Swedes bought seven books for every one a Spaniard bought. The Reformation's demand that everyone read scripture for themselves did more for mass literacy than the medieval universities did, which is why the books and schooling indicators part from university age in the north.

        Why it is included

        The chartered university was the third institution of the old Latin Roman facet. Measured separately, it tests whether the West's early universities still mark it off: they do in part, along the old line, but books and schooling follow the Reformation as much as Rome.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        • The first university. The founding year of the first university on the region's soil, counted in centuries to 2026, for every region but the Ashkenazim. Bologna gives northern Italy nine centuries; Luxembourg, whose university opened in 2003, has two decades.
        • Books. Books bought per head each year in the second half of the eighteenth century, from Eltjo Buringh and Jan Luiten van Zanden's reconstruction of manuscript and print output, on a log scale; twelve countries. The Dutch bought half a book per head a year, the Russians one for every hundred and seventy people.
        • Schooling in 1910. Average years of schooling of the adult population, from Jong-Wha Lee and Hanol Lee's long-run series: the United States 6.9, Norway 6.0, Romania 0.3, Albania 0.1. Their figure for Luxembourg, 0.17 years, a fifteenth of every neighbour's, is left out as implausible.

        Further cases

        Slovenia and Luxembourg. The rule counts the first university on a region's own soil, and that punishes small nations inside empires: Slovenes studied in Vienna and Graz for centuries before Ljubljana's university opened in 1919, Luxembourgers in Louvain and Paris before 2003. The age of a university measures sovereignty and size as well as learning. It is the crudest indicator in the atlas, and where it is the only one, as for Slovenia, the facet is only as good as it.
        Constantinople. A state school of higher learning from 425 and a law school from 1045, both older than Bologna. They belonged to the emperor, not to their masters, and they did not seed others. The difference the facet records is not learning but its corporate form.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes. Learning is handed down through named institutions, taken up by each generation as a task, and losable: universities have been closed, purged and refounded — Prague's split by language in 1882, Kraków's professors deported in 1939 — and literacy has risen and fallen with the states that taught it.

        Civic trust

        Whether a stranger can be trusted: the habit corporations and courts depend on

        Corporate personality, the second institution of the Latin package, lets strangers pool money, sue and be sued, and bind each other across generations. Such bodies work only where people expect strangers to keep their word. The facet measures that expectation directly: the share of people who answer that "most people can be trusted", rather than "you can't be too careful", in the World Values Survey and the European Values Study, the longest-running comparative survey of values.

        Dialectics

        Denmark and Albania. Seventy-four per cent against three: the two ends of the atlas. Both are small, both were poor within living memory; one trusts strangers more than almost anywhere on earth, the other less.

        Why it is included

        Trust among strangers is what the corporation, the court and the contract of the Latin package presuppose, and what the kinship argument predicts the Church's marriage rules produced. It is in the atlas to test that: where the old Latin line and today's trust disagree — Belarus above France, Portugal below Austria — the facet says so.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        The latest wave for each country, 2017–22 for most; Belgium, Ireland, Luxembourg and Moldova from 2010, Israel from 2004. Regions inside a country take its figure; Ibero-America is the population-weighted mean of seven countries; the Ashkenazim have no figure.

        Further cases

        Belarus. Orthodox, east of the Hajnal line, and under one ruler since 1994, yet forty per cent of Belarusians say most people can be trusted — more than the French or the Italians. A survey of trust measures something real, but not one thing: trust in strangers and trust in institutions part company under autocracy.
        Northern and southern Italy. Robert Putnam traced the gap between the institutions of northern and southern Italy to the medieval communes of the north and the kingdom of the south. The atlas cannot see it: the survey figure is Italy's, so the Mezzogiorno and Milan share one number. National figures for regions are the weakest point of this facet.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes, though it is the facet furthest from a heritage. Trust is taught by experience — by courts that enforce and officials who do not take bribes — and lost the same way. It is the clearest case in the atlas of a transmitted facet that is also an outcome of everything else.

        Individualism

        The trait most often claimed for the West, measured

        Individualism, in the sense psychologists measure, is how far people define themselves by their own attributes and choices rather than by the groups they belong to — family, clan, village — which look after them in exchange for loyalty. Geert Hofstede built the first comparative index from surveys of IBM employees in the late 1960s and 1970s, and it has been extended and replicated since. On it, the English-speaking world comes first by a distance, north-western Europe next, and the Balkans, Portugal and Latin America last.

        Dialectics

        Kinship and the self. Schulz and colleagues find that the longer a population lived under the Western Church, the weaker its kinship and the more individualist it is today. In this atlas Family & kinship and Individualism agree moderately — see the corroboration table — and the exceptions are as instructive as the rule: Hungary individualist east of the Hajnal line, Portugal collectivist west of it.

        Why it is included

        Individualism is the trait most often claimed to define the West, and the one the kinship argument says the Church's marriage rules produced. It is in the atlas to test both claims. It follows the north-west to south-east gradient more than the church line, and on the headline voices it is one of the facets that moves Croatia and Slovenia east.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        How it is measured

        Hofstede's individualism index, 0 to 100, from his dataset of August 2015. Québec takes the French-Canadian score; the regions inside Germany, Italy and Britain take their country's; Ibero-America is the population-weighted mean of seven countries. There is no score for Iceland, Belarus, Ukraine, Moldova, Bosnia, Montenegro, Macedonia or Albania, nor for the Ashkenazim, and those regions are left out of this facet. The scores for the Balkans and parts of central Europe come from later replications and are rougher than the rest.

        Further cases

        Hungary. 80, the same as the Netherlands, and far from every neighbour: Austria 55, Slovakia 52, Romania 30. Either Hungary is genuinely an outlier or the measure is; Hofstede's critics take it as evidence for the second, and it is a reason to treat any one region's score with caution.
        WEIRD. Joseph Henrich's acronym — Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic — names the populations psychology studied most, which turn out to be outliers on individualism, analytic thinking and trust in strangers. The English-speaking world is the outlier's outlier: the United States, Australia and Britain top this facet. Alan Macfarlane argued in 1978 that English individualism is older than capitalism, visible in the land markets of the thirteenth century.

        Genealogical interest, named but not scored

        Keeping track of one's ancestors is a candidate trait too, and this atlas is itself an instance of it. It is left unscored because no source measures it comparably across countries: membership of genealogy sites follows marketing and language, and search interest is published only as relative indices. What can be measured is the record that makes genealogy possible, and it is unevenly old. England ordered every parish to keep a register of baptisms, marriages and burials in 1538; the Council of Trent ordered it for every Catholic parish in 1563; Sweden's Church Law of 1686 made its registers the most complete in Europe; Russia's metrical books began under Peter the Great in the 1720s. The Lutheran and Anglican state churches kept the fullest records, which is why Nordic and British family histories can usually be traced further back than most. A people's interest in its lineage is real; whether it marks the West, or only marks where the West kept paperwork, the atlas cannot yet say.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes, uneasily. A way of seeing oneself is handed down through families, schools and law, taken up or not, and losable. But individualism is the facet most entangled with wealth: richer countries score higher, and part of what the index measures is the century of economic history behind each country rather than a heritage.

        Art

        Where the Gothic-to-Baroque building tradition took root

        The facet codes one thing: whether the Gothic-to-Baroque building tradition took root in a region's own churches, town halls and palaces. Five values: both Gothic and Baroque; Gothic only; Baroque only, without a medieval Gothic behind it; revival only, where the style arrived as nineteenth-century Gothic Revival; and neither. Each region is coded by its core territory, and the exceptions are named below.

        Revival only is about the medieval layer, not about whether Gothic or Baroque buildings stand there now: plenty do. The settler societies did not exist when Gothic was built, so what they have is a retrieval — Pugin's Westminster exported, St Patrick's Cathedral in New York, Washington National Cathedral, the Gothic quadrangles of their universities — a style consciously taken up again from a past the builders never lived in. The complication is Spanish colonial Baroque inside the modern United States — San Xavier del Bac in Arizona, the Texas missions, St Louis Cathedral in New Orleans — genuine Baroque, built in the Ibero-American and French traditions rather than the Anglo-American one, on land the United States later acquired.

        Architecture was chosen over painting deliberately. Coding sacred images directly — naturalistic figure against icon against aniconism — would largely restate Confession and count the same event twice. Buildings are a separate record: they are expensive, durable, datable, and still standing to be checked.

        Dialectics

        Saint-Denis and Gratian, 1140. Abbot Suger's choir at Saint-Denis, the first Gothic building, went up between 1140 and 1144 — the same years Gratian was compiling the Decretum at Bologna. Gothic and canon law were born in the same decade inside the same institutional world, and the cathedral chapters that commissioned Gothic were exactly the corporate bodies canon law had just created. That is why Art agreed with the old Latin Roman coding more closely than with anything else, and why it still follows Confession today.

        Why it is included

        A building tradition is expensive, durable and still standing, so it can be checked on the ground in a way few cultural markers can. Gothic and Baroque are the two styles that travelled with the Latin institutions, and whether each took root in a region's own churches and town halls records which inheritance reached it, and when. The buildings on Plate I are the evidence.

        More: further cases, Heidegger's test, sources

        Further cases

        Baroque without Gothic. The eastern frontier took the later layer and skipped the earlier one. Cossack Baroque came to Kyiv through Poland-Lithuania, and Rastrelli's St Andrew's Church stands above the Dnieper. Peter and Elizabeth brought Baroque to St Petersburg. Wallachia built its own Baroque synthesis, the Brâncovenesc style, and Serbian churches in the Habsburg Vojvodina took Baroque towers and iconostases. Baroque travelled east with Polish, Habsburg and Petrine power; Gothic, which travelled with the canon-law chapters, never did.
        The synagogue that chose not to look like a church. When nineteenth-century Jewish communities built monumental synagogues — Dohány Street in Budapest, 1859; the Neue Synagoge in Berlin, 1866 — they mostly chose Moorish Revival, deliberately avoiding Gothic forms that would read as church. In Israel, the Crusader Gothic halls of Acre stand as ruins of a tradition that never took root.

        Heidegger's test

        Passes. A building tradition is handed down through masons' lodges, workshops and academies, taken up or refused by each patron and each generation, and losable: the Dutch iconoclasm of 1566 stripped the churches of the Low Countries in weeks. The Anglosphere revival is the facet's clearest case of a heritage deliberately taken up again.

        Foodways

        What a region actually eats and drinks, measured rather than coded

        Every other transmitted facet is coded by judgement from the historical sources. This one is measured from official statistics, and it has three parts. Drink: the share of all alcohol drunk as beer, as wine and as spirits, from the World Health Organization's 2024 report, averaged over 2017 to 2019 — the measure behind the old "drinking belts" of Europe. Fat: the share of fat calories from animal fat — butter, lard, tallow — against vegetable oil, from the FAO's food balance sheets. Starch: how starch calories divide between wheat, maize, rice, other cereals such as rye, oats and barley, and potatoes and other roots, from the same tables. Each part is a composition, compared by the Hellinger distance, and the facet is their average.

        Festive calendar

        The holidays the law keeps, who brings the presents, and on which night the family sits down

        The statute book. Half of the facet is the religious and seasonal public holidays each region's law sets for 2026, compared by the share two regions hold in common. Bavaria has nine, Greece eight including Clean Monday, Russia one — the Julian Christmas — and the United States one. Corpus Christi marks the Catholic lands, from Portugal and Croatia to Poland and Bavaria; Reformation Day, Slovenia and the German east; Saints Cyril and Methodius, Bohemia, Slovakia, Bulgaria and Macedonia; Midsummer, Finland, Sweden, the Baltic states and Québec; the Eids, Albania, Bosnia and Macedonia. In federations a holiday counts by the share of the population whose state keeps it, so the Reformation counts for most of north Germany and Corpus Christi for about a fifth of it.

        The household. The other half codes two customs nearly every household keeps. The first is who brings the children's main presents: a Father Christmas figure — Santa Claus, Père Noël, the Scandinavian Tomte and Nisse, Finland's Joulupukki, Romania's Moș Crăciun, Bulgaria's Dyado Koleda; the Christ Child — the Christkind of southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland, Ježíšek in Bohemia, Jézuska in Hungary; St Nicholas on 5 or 6 December in the Low Countries and Galicia; the Three Kings on 6 January in Spain; Grandfather Frost at New Year across the former socialist east; or St Basil on New Year's Day in Greece. The second is when the principal Christmas observance falls: Christmas Eve or Christmas Day by the Gregorian calendar, or 6–7 January by the Julian.

        Dialectics

        The Christ Child, a Protestant invention. The Christkind was promoted by Luther's circle to move present-giving from the feast of St Nicholas, a saint, to Christmas itself. Five centuries later it is the gift-bringer of the Catholic south — Bavaria, Austria, Bohemia, Hungary — while Protestant north Germany has the Weihnachtsmann. A custom crossed the confessional line it was invented on.

        Why it is included

        The calendar is the most widely shared ritual in a household's year: who brings the presents and on which night the family sits down together. Both are transmitted, both are taught to children, and both have been changed by churches and states within living memory. It is in the atlas because it is the everyday trace of confession — and because, where it parts from confession, it shows what else a people hands down.

        Further cases

        Grandfather Frost. Soviet policy restored the New Year tree in the mid-1930s and gave it Ded Moroz, a secular gift-bringer for a secular holiday.         Dress, named but not scored

        Folk dress is the other everyday marker most people would name, and it is left unscored because no source codes it consistently across the atlas. It shows the same pattern of loss and revival: Highland dress was banned by the Dress Act of 1746 and revived in the nineteenth century as Scotland's national costume; the Norwegian bunad is largely a national-romantic creation of the early twentieth century; the Ukrainian embroidered vyshyvanka has become a sign of national allegiance in the present one. Each is a heritage deliberately taken up again.

        The Latin package: a hypothesis, tested by its parts

        Four medieval legal inventions — did they travel together?

        Until version 1.5 this was a facet: a judgement, coded by hand, of whether four legal institutions arrived in a region together — consent marriage under canon law, corporate personality, the chartered university, and the enforceable contract of the revived Roman law — scored inside, frontier or outside.         Why they arrived together

        Why they arrived together. Harold Berman's argument in Law and Revolution is that the founding event is the Papal Revolution — Gregory VII's break with the emperor and the Investiture Controversy that followed, roughly 1075 to 1122. The Church declared itself a jurisdiction independent of secular rulers, and to govern itself it built the first modern legal system in the West: a systematic body of law, professional courts, trained jurists, an appellate hierarchy. Secular rulers then had to build competing systems to match it. Western constitutionalism starts there, in the plain fact that for the first time there were two jurisdictions over the same people, and each could check the other.

        Further cases

        Cousin marriage in India and the Arab world. The individual-consent rule never arrived, and the canon-law cousin bans never applied. In India marriage remains overwhelmingly an alliance negotiated between lineages, with caste endogamy the organising constraint, separate personal-law regimes by community, and cross-cousin and uncle-niece marriage preferred across much of the south. In much of the Arab world the preferred match is a man's father's brother's daughter, the bint 'amm. Both are precisely the arrangements canon law spent four centuries prohibiting, for precisely the reason it prohibited them: they keep property and alliance inside the lineage, and they produce what the canon lawyers were dismantling — dense, self-reinforcing kin groups that remain the primary unit of trust and political loyalty.
        The waqf and the corporation. Islamic law developed a perpetual charitable endowment, the waqf, which genuinely holds property in perpetuity. But a waqf is not a legal person: it cannot sue or be sued, cannot contract, has no members, and its purpose is frozen at founding. Timur Kuran's argument in The Long Divergence is that the absence of corporate personality, not any deficit of commerce or capital, prevented durable large-scale enterprise. Everything except the persona ficta, and the firms do not form.
        Why the package satisfies Heidegger's criterion.

        Being and Time §74 defines a collective by Geschick: a heritage handed down (Überlieferung) and taken over as a task through resolute repetition (Wiederholung). Not a property of a thing — a happening that people are inside of.

        It is transmitted, not possessed. Gratian taught at Bologna, Bologna trained the canonists, the canonists staffed the courts, the courts made the rulings, the rulings were copied into the manuals that trained the next generation. You can name the teachers.

        Membership is by taking it up. Peru enters in the 1530s by receiving the package and is fully inside; so do Philadelphia, Montreal and Sydney later. Birth confers nothing here.

        It is losable — the test both birthright facets fail absolutely. A polity that abandons judicial independence, subordinates its universities, or stops enforcing contracts against the powerful has dropped out of the transmission whatever its population's genome says.

        The honest limit. §74 shows this is a Geschick. It cannot show that it is yours — Heidegger never supplies a criterion for which heritage is properly one's own, and that vacancy is exactly what got filled by the state in 1933.

        Conclusion

        The Latin Roman West, the Byzantine East and the Hebraic Jews are emergent and quantifiable. Their cores persist under every test; their borders do not, and the seam regions go with whichever kind of facet gets more say (Plate IV). Every facet that draws them — a creed, a household form, a school, a habit of trusting strangers — can be received by someone not born to it, and lost by someone who was.

        Sardinia, with almost no steppe ancestry, is fully inside the Latin West; so are Québec, Sydney and Philadelphia, for the same reason Kraków is. Lima and Mexico City took the Latin church, law and Baroque, yet on trust and individualism sit nearer Bucharest than Madrid. And what can be taken up can be let go: Fraktur abolished by memo in 1941, Moldova's alphabet switched twice in fifty years, Ireland's vernacular lost in three generations. Nothing here is owed by birth or closed by it. The inheritances are many, and each can be taken up, so anyone can join them in a combination no one else holds and make themselves an example of their own. In every age, each of us is headstrong enough to choose. Protect what you love, and take it up yourself.

        Philosophy

        Aristotle’s History of Animals keeps a strict order of inquiry. It first gathers the facts — the parts of animals, their ways of life, their activities and characters — and leaves their causes to later works, Parts of Animals and Generation of Animals. The differences and attributes of every animal must be grasped first, he writes, and only then their causes sought (I.6, 491a). The Posterior Analytics names the two kinds of knowledge: knowing that a thing is so, to hoti (τὸ ὅτι), and knowing why it is so, to dioti (τὸ διότι). The plates above are this atlas’s hoti: what peoplehood consists of, measured. What follows is its dioti, the author’s account of why it matters. It comes after the facts, and the facts do not depend on it.

        As the means of securing a rooted identity and safeguarding the distinctiveness of a people, love of one’s own serves a higher human purpose. In this reading, such patriotism is an antidote to the “selfish” ideologies that dominate modern cultural life, ideologies that call themselves liberating but leave only dissolution.

        The first is the post-war dogma of perpetual penance. It places Germany, and by extension the West, at the moral centre of the world, yet requires it to remain spiritually disarmed. Its identity is permitted only as confession and its past only as indictment, so that no confident self-understanding can disturb the managed consensus. This arrangement is inherently unstable, a kind of moral armistice rather than reconciliation. It is not the lasting harmony of a renewed ethical order, because a people that may only repent can never truly be at peace with itself or its neighbours.

        The second is the global market. Platform capitalism and consumer monoculture erode the ability of communities to govern their own internal lives, and a corporate acquisitiveness turns identity itself into a product to be branded, sold, and audited. It is the old colonial appetite in new form: not territory but attention, taste, and belonging are annexed.

        Finally, there is the attempt to establish a new universal moral regime. Promoted through universities, NGOs, and the human-resources department, the “Woke” Postmodernist Diversity Inclusion & Equity (D.I.E.) apparatus imposes a single code of speech and value across every culture it touches. It treats every inherited tradition as a structure of power to be unmasked. Yet having dissolved the old authorities, it installs its own: a single vocabulary of identity, privilege, and grievance. It negates plurality in the very name of diversity, replacing living spiritual traditions with one administered orthodoxy.

        These doctrines are a two-dimensional cosmopolitanism, or its alienated double: a nihilism that deconstructs every inherited form of meaning and offers nothing in its place. By short-cutting, undermining, and flattening particular peoples, they conflict with the loftier interests of humanity, which is only ever realised through the flourishing of distinct peoples, not their dissolution.

        The remedy is not another doctrine but a different kind of person, and so it begins with education. Its task is to raise people who govern themselves: good at the root, who want what is right and do it of their own accord, without hesitating or wavering, expecting no reward and indifferent to the arithmetic of advantage. Such people are not made by exhortation. They are formed indirectly, by freeing the native drive to think for oneself. A school must therefore teach not what to think, but how to think.

        Facts committed to memory matter less than the mind’s own play and the joy of learning for its own sake. That spontaneity, and the love that comes with it, is the sine qua non of moral life; it is the same love that, turned toward one’s own people, becomes patriotism. The true end of education is a student who can picture for himself the good order of a life and of a people, and who, seized by love for that picture, wills it and works to make it real.
